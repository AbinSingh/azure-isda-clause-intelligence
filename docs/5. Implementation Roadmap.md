What will this file do?

Every clause extracted from a PDF needs a consistent representation.

For example, Section 2(d)(i) should retain:

    Its clause number.
    Its heading.
    Its actual text from the PDF.
    Its parent clause.
    Its page numbers.
    Its child clauses, if any.
    Its document identifier.

We'll use Python dataclasses for this model. They're lightweight and don't require an additional dependency.

# 3. Why use a JSON registry?

There are three main benefits.

A. Consistent clause identification

A clause can have the same internal ID across all your agreements.

For example:

    {
      "clause_id": "section_2_d_i",
      "clause_number": "2(d)(i)",
      "clause_title": "Gross-Up"
    }

Your application can use section_2_d_i as a stable identifier even when the actual text differs between two agreements.

B. Parent-child relationships

The registry tells your parser that 2(d)(i) belongs to 2(d), which belongs to Section 2.

This will help your retrieval system return a complete, relevant clause with its necessary context.

C. Easier maintenance

If you later discover that a heading needs to be corrected or additional subsections need to be registered, 
you can update the JSON file without rewriting your Python parsing logic.

# Why build lookup indexes?

Without indexes, the application would need to traverse the nested JSON hierarchy every time it wanted to find a clause.

The loader builds two dictionaries:

clauses_by_id: lookup by internal ID, such as section_2_d_i.
clauses_by_number: lookup by clause number, such as 2(d)(i).

These make lookups straightforward.

Why are we preserving so much metadata?

Because later, we'll need to answer questions such as:

    Which clause does this paragraph belong to?
    On which page did the clause begin?
    Does the clause continue onto another page?
    Is the heading actually a title or just ordinary text?
    Can we trace a retrieved chunk back to its original location?

Preserving spans and bounding regions also allows us to investigate extraction problems without repeatedly 
processing the original PDF.

Important: This parser does not yet infer clause boundaries. It only normalizes the Azure result so the next stage 
can do that reliably.

4.1 Our approach

We'll use three things together:

    The clause registry from Step 2 to identify known section and clause headings.
    The normalized paragraphs from Step 3 as the source text.
    Numbering patterns and paragraph roles to identify clause boundaries.

For now, we'll support the hierarchy we've registered:

    1. Interpretation
    (a) Definitions
    (b) Inconsistency
    (c) Single Agreement

    2. Obligations
        (a) General Conditions
            (i) ...
            (ii) ...
            (iii) ...
        (b) Change of Account
        (c) Netting of Payments
        (d) Deduction or Withholding for Tax
            (i) Gross-Up

Notice that (i) can occur under more than one parent clause. Therefore, we must use the current parent clause to 
determine its meaning rather than treating (i) as a globally unique identifier.

# Step 4.1 — Define how clause matching should work

Based on your extracted paragraphs, our matcher needs to recognize four structural levels:
    
    | Level | Example                       | Meaning            |
    | ----- | ----------------------------- | ------------------ |
    | 0     | `2. Obligations`              | Main section       |
    | 1     | `(a) General Conditions.`     | Subsection         |
    | 2     | `(i) Each party will make...` | Numbered provision |
    | 3     | `(1) promptly notify...`      | Nested provision   |

The matcher will use the numbering patterns and the registry to identify these levels.

Important observations from your PDF
    1. Section headings: Paragraphs 6 and 10 identify Sections 1 and 2.
    2. Subsections: Paragraphs 7–9 and 11, 16–17, and 22 contain lettered subsections.
    3. Continued provisions: Paragraphs 15 and 20 are parts of Section 2(a)(iii). The second paragraph continues the same provision.
    4. Nested provisions: Paragraphs 24–26 are numbered items (1), (2), and (3) under Section 2(d)(i).
    5. Non-clause content: Paragraphs 14, 27, and 28 are footer or page-number elements and should not be included in legal clause text.
    6. Subsection continuation: Paragraphs 18 and 19 contain (i) and (ii) under Section 2(c), rather than new top-level sections.

# Step 4.3 — Recommended matcher design

I recommend separating clause recognition from clause construction.

The matcher should:

    Read the normalized paragraphs from document_parser.py.
    Ignore page headers, footers, and page numbers.
    Recognize main section headings such as 2. Obligations.
    Recognize subsection headings such as (d) Deduction or Withholding for Tax.
    Track the current section and subsection while iterating through paragraphs.
    Identify nested provisions such as (i) and (1).
    Append continuation paragraphs to the correct parent provision.
    Record the starting and ending pages of each clause.
    Return structured clause objects using your existing Clause dataclass.

# A key architectural decision

Your registry should describe the expected legal hierarchy, but the PDF should determine the actual text and 
observed structure.

This distinction matters because different agreements may contain amendments, omitted provisions, or other variations.
We should not silently manufacture missing clauses or assume every document has identical text.
